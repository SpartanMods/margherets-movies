# Pay It Forward (2000) Review: The Opposite of an Eye for an Eye

Pay It Forward is a perspective-changing movie. Taken to heart, it can change the way you see the world.

That's a big claim for a 2000 drama about a seventh grader's homework assignment, and I don't make it lightly. The movie isn't perfect. It's slow in places, and the ending makes a choice I still argue with. But I walked away thinking about how I treat people, which is more than most movies manage. My score: 88 out of 100.

## A homework assignment that got out of hand

The setup is simple. Trevor McKinney (Haley Joel Osment) is starting seventh grade in Las Vegas. His social studies teacher, Eugene Simonet (Kevin Spacey), gives the class an assignment: come up with an idea to change the world, then put it into action.

Most kids would write a paper. Trevor builds a system. Do something big for three people, something they can't do for themselves. Instead of paying you back, each of them does the same for three more people. The good spreads.

That's the whole engine of the movie, and the idea alone is worth the two hours.

## Big, not nice

What makes it land is a single word in Trevor's rule: big.

People do little things for each other all the time. Hold a door, buy a coffee, cover a shift. And a lot of the time, if we're honest, we expect something back. A favor gets banked. Help comes with a receipt.

Pay It Forward asks something harder. Sit down and actually think about what you could do that would change someone's life. Do it out of the goodness of your soul. Then, instead of asking them to pay you back, ask them to do something good for someone else.

The twist is that it isn't really about making the world a nicer place in the abstract. You're doing something for another person to help make that person a better person. And you become a better person in the process. It's the opposite of an eye for an eye making the whole world blind. One good turn makes three more people see.

That's why it's perspective-shifting. It quietly calls out the kind of helping that is really self-interest in a nicer outfit.

## The ingenuity of a kid

Trevor's first move is to bring a homeless man, Jerry (Jim Caviezel), into the house without telling his mom, then play it off with a straight face. It's funny, a little reckless, and completely sincere.

That scene says something the whole movie keeps returning to. Kids have a creativity and wit that a lot of adults have lost. Somewhere along the way most of us stop asking whether the world has to be the way it is. Trevor never got the memo.

## Arlene: from "he's just a kid" to standing with him

Helen Hunt plays Arlene, Trevor's mom, a single mother working two jobs and trying to stay sober. Her arc is the best thing in the movie.

At first she sees none of what Trevor is doing. When she finds a stranger in her house, she goes straight to the teacher to demand what he did to make her son do this. In her eyes, Trevor's just a kid who doesn't know what he's doing.

The shift comes slowly. Trevor sets her up with Eugene, and the second time around it actually works. You can see her start to respect him.

The full-circle moment comes when her ex-husband Ricky (Jon Bon Jovi) shows up again and, in one of his drunken outbursts, she locks herself in the room with Trevor and admits she made a mistake. It's just a few words and one small action. But in that moment she stands with her son instead of the man who hurt them both, and she closes the door on a life she no longer has to live. It's subtle and powerful, and it shows how much Trevor has helped her become a better version of herself.

## Eugene and the box he keeps himself in

Kevin Spacey's Eugene is a sharp, careful man. Everything he says to Trevor and to Arlene is chosen, and more than once his words hit harder than you expect. You can tell there's a lot of intelligence and passion in him. It's just locked in a box very far away.

What I liked is that he grows too. Through Trevor and Arlene he comes out of his shell and realizes he wants a fuller life, instead of letting chances slide by and being one of those drones that just keeps on keeping on. The kid changes the teacher, not the other way around.

Here's my one gripe with him: he doesn't actually drive much of the story. Trevor does almost all of it. The moment Trevor needs Eugene to step up, when Ricky comes back, Eugene ends up out the door instead. It's a heart-dropping moment, the first time you wonder whether this story is going to end happily at all. But it also leaves Eugene as more of a passenger than a player.

## Where it drags

This isn't a movie that blows you off your seat. It's slow in stretches, and it takes its time getting where it's going. It's easy to get into and it moves along well enough, but if you need momentum, you'll feel the runtime.

The filmmaking is solid rather than flashy. One smart choice is how it cuts in and out of the story: a reporter, Chris Chandler (Jay Mohr), traces the chain of good deeds backward until he finds where it started. That structure pays off later in a big way.

## The world, then and now

Trevor doesn't sugarcoat it. As far as he's concerned, the world kind of sucks, and somebody has to do something about it.

That lands even harder today. We have more distractions than ever, more ways to hurt each other and actually feel good about it. There's war, and there are a lot of people out for themselves. One small change in thinking, from "what can I get out of this?" to "what can I do for someone that they can't do for themselves?", could genuinely help the world right now. I believe that.

What makes Trevor work is his humility. He's not out to be a hero. He just thinks it's worth a shot.

## Spoiler warning: the ending

&#91;SPOILERS START\]

Trevor dies. He steps in to protect his friend Adam from bullies, and one of them stabs him. He doesn't make it.

The movie then replays his interview on TV, and it hits completely differently the second time. Watching his words again after he's gone is easily the most powerful stretch of the film. Thousands of people show up at the house to honor him.

Honestly, I don't think the death was necessary. The stabbing felt a little forced, like the movie reached for tragedy to make sure you'd cry. It does drive the message home harder. I didn't hate it. But I think there were better ways to land it.

Here's what I would have done. Let Trevor actually save Adam, then time-lapse through Adam's own pay-it-forward journey, so the chain keeps going. Or pan out to news reports showing the movement spreading. Not to make Trevor famous, but to show the world actually getting better. A wider, macro look at what it could look like if people just paid it forward and did the right thing. That's the movie's idea at full scale, and I would've loved to see it.

## Who it's for

This is for people who want a high-quality, well-thought-out movie that leaves a real impact on you when you get up off the couch. It's not an action or comedy thrill ride, and it won't give you chills from fear. It's a movie that makes you think, and it leaves you feeling like you've learned something.

If you want pace and spectacle, skip it. If you're open to having your perspective shifted, press play.
