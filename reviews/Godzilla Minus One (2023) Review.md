# Godzilla Minus One (2023): Review

Score: 97/100

Directed and written by Takashi Yamazaki. Starring Ryunosuke Kamiki, Minami Hamabe, Munetaka Aoki, Hidetaka Yoshioka and Yuki Yamada. Score by Naoki Satō. 125 minutes. Toho.

## The short version

Godzilla Minus One kicks the modern blockbuster on its ass. Toho made it for under $15 million, which wouldn't cover the catering on some Hollywood monster movies, and it still walked away with the Oscar for Best Visual Effects. Honestly, it disgraced big Hollywood. But the budget isn't what got me. What got me is that the film actually gives a damn about the people running from the monster.

I'd call it a postwar drama first and a Godzilla movie second, and I mean that as the highest compliment I can give a kaiju flick.

## The story

Yamazaki opens on a kamikaze pilot who chickened out, not on the monster, and this one choice sets up the whole movie. Every time Godzilla surfaced after that, I wasn't waiting to see what got flattened. I was waiting to see whether Kōichi would freeze again. His ambivalence, the pull between wanting to live and believing he shouldn't, is the engine of the entire film.

Setting it in 1947 does a lot of the heavy lifting. Japan has no real military left, the government is keeping the public in the dark, and the Americans aren't coming. So the usual Godzilla playbook of tanks and fighter jets is off the table, and the job falls to a handful of veterans and civilians who have already lost almost everything. The title fits perfectly. The country is starting from zero, and Godzilla knocks it down to minus one.

I also like how the attacks are built around Kōichi instead of around spectacle. Odo Island is where his guilt comes from. Ginza takes away the life he'd been half-heartedly building with Noriko. By the finale he's stuck with the question he's been dodging since 1945: does he stay the coward who didn't fly, or does he finally make it right? Most monster movies don't bother to give their human lead anything to decide.

Story and writing: 20/20. I honestly couldn't find anything to dock. Not every character gets a full arc. Noriko stays pretty steady the whole way through. But nobody is dead weight, either. Tachibana, Noda, Captain Akitsu and the kid Mizushima each pull Kōichi a little further out of his hole, and his relationship with Noriko grows slowly enough that you buy it.

## Execution

Apart from two moments I'll get to, the execution is impeccable. The Odo Island attack plays like a horror movie: night, darkness, and a T-Rex-sized creature picking men off one by one. It's horrific and trauma-inducing. Ginza goes full scale, and it's the epitome of what a Godzilla attack should feel like. This isn't a Marvel movie where the city has conveniently been evacuated and buildings come down without anyone getting hurt. The camera stays on the people being crushed, burned and scattered by the sheer devastation Godzilla brings. When those dorsal plates start clicking up and that blue glow builds, there's nothing subtle about what's coming. The blast is in your face: explosive, devastating and cataclysmic. It's so well done that people in the theater actually gasped.

Execution: 18/20. I took two points off for plot armor. The first is the train in Ginza. Godzilla bites the car, Noriko hangs off the edge, drops into the river and is basically fine. Fun to watch, hard to believe. The second is the heat ray. It goes off like a mushroom cloud over the district, and somehow people standing right in it get back up and walk around in the black rain. I'll give a Godzilla movie some slack, but that's a lot of slack. I did love how Godzilla shows off his regeneration after the mine goes off in his mouth. It's an ingeniously subtle way to flex that power.

## Performances

Performances: 20/20.

You'll hear people call the acting theatrical, mostly Ryunosuke Kamiki's breakdowns as Kōichi. I don't buy it. He's playing a guy who was supposed to die for his country, didn't, and then came home to find the people he was meant to protect dead anyway, his parents included. He thinks he has no right to be alive. Honestly, I think his outbursts could have been bigger.

What Kamiki nails is how passive Kōichi is. Things just happen to him. Noriko moves in and he lets her. He ends up looking after a baby that isn't his. On the surface it reads like a man making amends, but I don't think it is, at least not early on. He goes along with it because he doesn't believe he has the right to say no. He can't sleep, he sees Godzilla every night, and he keeps Noriko at arm's length because getting close would mean admitting he wants to live. Kamiki plays all of that with almost no dialogue, and there's a quiet eloquence to it.

Munetaka Aoki's Tachibana is my other favorite. He spends half the movie hating Kōichi, and then shows up anyway because he can see Kōichi's war never ended. That turn works because Aoki never plays it soft. Blockbusters usually cut away from this kind of grief. This one sits in it.

## Impact

Impact: 30/30.

This is where most of the score comes from. You can watch it as an action movie and have a great time. I got a lot more out of it watching it as a film about what war leaves behind, and how evil it is.

Tokyo has been firebombed flat, and Hiroshima and Nagasaki happened only two years earlier. People are rebuilding from scratch and don't get any time to grieve, so they bottle it up, and you see it come out sideways in someone like Kōichi. Godzilla himself was mutated by American nuclear tests in the Pacific. It's hard not to read him as the bill coming due for what countries were doing to each other.

What stuck with me more, though, is that it's a movie about second chances. It keeps giving people the chance to do the right thing and treating them with enough respect that they take it. When the government won't act, ordinary people do: ex-navy men in disarmed destroyers, civilians in tugboats. Do wrong and the monster keeps coming back. Do right and you might actually get somewhere.

## Rewatchability

Rewatchability: 9/10.

It loses a point only because it's so memorable that you don't need a rewatch to recall the big moments. If you're a Godzilla fan, though, you could put this on every year. The destruction finally matches how colossal and radioactive he's supposed to be, and when Naoki Satō's score brings back Akira Ifukube's original theme, it's out of this world. I got chills.

## Final score

- Story and writing: 20/20
- Execution: 18/20
- Performances: 20/20
- Impact: 30/30
- Rewatchability: 9/10

Total: 97/100

## Spoiler section

Stop here if you haven't seen it.

The ending is worth slowing down on. Noriko gets caught in the Ginza blast, and for the whole last act we think she's dead. Kōichi plans one last kamikaze run in a Shinden fighter, straight into Godzilla's mouth. What he doesn't know is that Tachibana fitted the plane with an ejection seat. He punches out and lives, and it's the first time in the movie he actually chooses to.

Then the telegram: Noriko survived and she's in the hospital. Godzilla movies almost never end this happy, so I was ready to enjoy it, until I noticed the dark mark creeping up her neck. Pair that with the last shot of Godzilla's remains regenerating as they sink, and I can't help wondering whether she's been infected with whatever mutated him. The movie doesn't say, and I like that it leaves it hanging.
